Near the coast and you feel a strong quake, or the sea pulls back?

  1. Go now. Don't wait for a siren, an app or the news. Nature's warning is often the only one.
  2. Go up and inland: at least 30 m (100 ft) high or 3 km (2 miles) inland. On foot if roads are jammed.
  3. No high ground near? Go to the 4th floor or higher of a strong concrete building (last resort).
  4. Stay there for hours. Waves come in a series and the first is often not the biggest. Wait for an official all-clear.
  5. Never go down to look at the sea or the retreating water.

Natural warning signs

"Long or strong, get gone."

  • Shaking that makes it hard to stand, or that lasts 20 seconds or more.
  • The sea suddenly drains away and exposes the seabed, reefs or fish (this happened in 2004).
  • A loud roar from the ocean, like a train or jet.
  • A fast rise of water or an unusual "wall" of water on the horizon.

A distant earthquake can send a tsunami with no shaking at all where you are. That's why official alerts and this app's push notifications matter too.

When you arrive at a beach hotel

  • Ask the front desk: "Where is the tsunami evacuation route and assembly point?"
  • Look for blue/white tsunami evacuation signs and walk the route once.
  • Find the nearest high ground or tall concrete building, and how long it takes to walk there.
  • Agree on a family meeting point on high ground, in case you are separated (e.g. one at the beach, one in the room).
  • Keep phones charged; carry ID, a copy of passports and a little cash in a small bag.
  • Save the local emergency numbers below and the hotel's phone number.

Before the trip

  • Turn on Emergency / Government alerts on every phone (iPhone: Settings → Notifications → bottom; Android: Settings → Safety & emergency → Wireless emergency alerts).
  • Install ntfy and subscribe to the family topic (see the README) so you get this app's alerts.
  • Install the official app of the country you're visiting (table below).
  • Share your itinerary and hotel names with someone at home.
  • Register your trip with your embassy if your country offers it.

During and after a quake

  • Drop, cover, hold on until the shaking stops. Then, if near the coast, evacuate immediately.
  • Expect aftershocks. Stay away from damaged buildings, bridges and power lines.
  • Use text messages instead of calls; networks overload.
  • If caught in water: grab something that floats. Don't try to swim against the current.

Other hazards: quick guide

Jump to: Cyclone · Flood · Heat & cold · Volcano · Landslide · Wildfire · Disease · Algae, sinkholes, avalanches, tornadoes

🌀 Tropical cyclone (hurricane / typhoon)

  • Check the season before booking: Atlantic/Caribbean Jun–Nov, NW Pacific (Philippines, Japan, S. China) mostly Jun–Dec, North Indian Ocean Apr–Jun and Oct–Dec, Southern Hemisphere Nov–Apr.
  • When a storm is forecast: follow evacuation orders, stock water, food, cash, medicines and charge phones.
  • Stay indoors in an interior room away from windows. The calm "eye" is not the end: the wind returns from the other side.
  • Storm surge (sea flooding) kills most people. Beach-front and low-lying rooms are the most dangerous.

🌊 Floods & flash floods

  • Turn around, don't drown. 15 cm of moving water can knock you over, 30 cm can float a car.
  • Flash floods hit within minutes of heavy rain, even far downstream. Avoid canyons, dry riverbeds and river camps when rain is forecast.
  • Move to higher floors or ground; stay away from power lines and drains.

🌡️ Extreme heat & cold

  • Heat: plan outings for early morning and evening, drink before you're thirsty, use shade and air-conditioning, and check on children and older relatives.
  • Heat stroke is an emergency: hot skin, confusion, fainting or seizures. Call the emergency number and cool the person with water and fanning.
  • Cold: dress in layers, cover the head and hands, and keep dry. Shivering, confusion or slurred speech can mean hypothermia.

🌋 Volcanic eruption

  • Respect exclusion zones, even if the volcano looks calm.
  • Ash fall: stay indoors with windows shut, wear an N95/FFP2 mask and goggles, and don't drive in thick ash.
  • Stay out of valleys below a volcano: lahars (volcanic mudflows) travel fast and far, especially in rain.
  • Eruptions can close airports for days. Keep travel insurance and flexible plans.

⛰️ Landslides & mudslides

  • Highest risk in steep areas during and after heavy rain or an earthquake.
  • Warning signs: new cracks in ground or walls, tilting trees or poles, sudden muddy water in streams, rumbling sounds.
  • If one starts, move sideways out of its path, uphill if you can. Avoid mountain roads in heavy rain.

🔥 Wildfire

  • Check fire danger and park closures before hiking or driving through forest and bush.
  • Leave early when told to; roads jam and fire moves faster than you can run uphill.
  • Smoke: stay indoors, close windows, use an N95/FFP2 mask outside.

🦠 Disease outbreaks & travel health

  • 4–6 weeks before: check the CDC destination page (wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel) or a travel clinic for vaccines and malaria tablets.
  • Mosquitoes (dengue, malaria, Zika, chikungunya): repellent with DEET, picaridin or IR3535, long sleeves at dawn and dusk, and nets or screened rooms.
  • Water & food: sealed bottled or boiled water; "boil it, cook it, peel it, or forget it".
  • Follow the level of the notice: CDC Level 3 = reconsider non-essential travel, Level 4 = avoid all travel.
  • A fever during or after the trip: see a doctor and say where you travelled.

Less common: algae, sinkholes, avalanches, tornadoes

  • Red tide / toxic algae: don't swim in discoloured or foul-smelling water or near dead fish, and skip local shellfish if there are warnings.
  • Sinkholes: rare and unpredictable; respect fenced or closed areas, especially near caves and karst coasts (e.g. Yucatán, Florida).
  • Avalanches: check the local avalanche bulletin before going off-piste. Carry a beacon, probe and shovel, and go with a guide.
  • Tornadoes (mainly central USA in spring): go to the lowest floor and an interior room without windows, and protect your head.

What this app can and can't do

  • Nobody can predict earthquakes. This app detects them after they happen and relays official tsunami bulletins, disaster alerts (GDACS), health notices (CDC/WHO) and weather forecasts.
  • The risk profile shows how likely each hazard is in a region over years, not a forecast for your exact days.
  • Alerts can arrive 5–30 minutes late (free scheduled checks). A tsunami near the source can arrive faster than that.
  • So: your own eyes and the natural signs come first, then local sirens and authorities, then this app.
